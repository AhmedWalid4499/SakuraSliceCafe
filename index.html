The Story Behind the Slices

Sakura Slice Café is a place that exists in the space between imagination and longing — a dream café born from a love of Japanese aesthetics, the warmth of slow mornings, and the belief that the right space can change how you feel about the day.

It's not (yet) a place you can walk into. But it lives in playlists, in morning letters, in the quiet hope that someday, there will be a corner of the world that feels exactly like this.

— Ahmed El-Bourgy, Dreamer

Matcha & Tea

Matcha Latte

Ceremonial grade, whisked by hand, never rushed. Hana will ask how sweet you want it and then make it slightly less sweet than you said, because she is usually right.

Made by Hana
How it's made ⌄
  1. Sift 2g of matcha through a fine strainer. Always sift — it's the whole difference between silk and lumps.
  2. Add 60ml of water at 80°C. Never boiling; boiling water makes matcha bitter and there is no fixing it afterward.
  3. Whisk in a brisk W shape, not a circle, for about 20 seconds — until the foam is fine-bubbled and holds its shape.
  4. Warm 180ml of milk to just under scalding and pour it down the inside of the cup so the foam survives.
  5. Sweeten only if asked, and then slightly less than they asked.
Hojicha Latte

Roasted, low in caffeine, the colour of an old photograph. What we drink after four when we want the feeling of coffee without the consequences of it.

Made by Hana
How it's made ⌄
  1. Steep 5g of roasted hojicha leaf in 80ml of water at 90°C for 90 seconds.
  2. Strain it out properly. The leaves keep giving and it turns papery past two minutes.
  3. Steam 200ml of milk and pour it in slowly — hojicha is delicate and a fast pour flattens it.
  4. No sugar. It doesn't need any, and Hana will tell you so if you ask.
Sakura Blossom Tea

Salt-preserved blossoms that open again in hot water. It tastes faintly of almond and slightly of spring. It is the most beautiful thing we serve and it takes four minutes.

Made by Hana
How it's made ⌄
  1. Rinse two salt-preserved blossoms briefly under cool water — take the edge off the salt, not all of it.
  2. Pour over 200ml of water at 80°C.
  3. Wait four minutes while the blossoms open. This part is not optional and cannot be hurried, which is arguably the point of the drink.
  4. Serve with the blossoms still floating in the cup. Never strain them out.
Genmaicha

Green tea with toasted rice. Tastes like a warm kitchen. Order this if you're staying a while — it's the one that refills for free.

Made by whoever's closest
How it's made ⌄
  1. 4g of genmaicha, 250ml of water at 80°C, 60 seconds.
  2. The same leaves take a second and third steep — add 10 seconds each time.
  3. Never squeeze the leaves when you strain. Squeezing is where bitterness comes from.
  4. Refill without being asked. That's the entire reason this one is on the menu.

Coffee

Sakura Cold Brew

Steeped eighteen hours, finished with a whisper of blossom syrup. Floral on the way in, clean on the way out. The drink most likely to make someone ask what you're having.

Made by Hana
How it's made ⌄
  1. 80g of coarsely ground coffee to 1 litre of cold water, in a sealed jar, in the fridge.
  2. Eighteen hours. Not twelve — it's thin. Not twenty-four — it's woody.
  3. Strain twice: once through mesh to get the grounds, once through paper to get the silt.
  4. Make the syrup by simmering 100g sugar, 100ml water and six rinsed blossoms for five minutes, then leaving it to cool with the blossoms still in.
  5. 15ml of syrup per glass, plenty of ice, stir exactly once.
The Long Morning

A flat white, but bigger, and served in the heavy cup. Named for the mornings Yuto spends at table four with a notebook and no particular plan.

Made by Hana
How it's made ⌄
  1. Pull a double ristretto — 18g in, about 25g out — straight into the heavy cup.
  2. Steam 150ml of whole milk to 60°C. Microfoam only: glossy, no visible bubbles.
  3. Swirl the jug, then pour from high to combine, drop low at the end to float the pattern.
  4. Serve without a spoon. It's already mixed and stirring only ruins the top.
Espresso

Standing at the counter, thirty seconds, no ceremony. Sometimes that's the correct amount of café for a person to have.

Made by Hana
How it's made ⌄
  1. 18g in the basket, distributed flat, tamped level. Level matters more than hard.
  2. 36g out in 27 seconds.
  3. If it runs fast, grind finer. If it chokes, grind coarser. That is the entire conversation.
  4. Wipe the spout and serve immediately — espresso has a shelf life of about a minute.
Turkish Coffee

Because we are, after all, in Cairo. Ask for it mazbout. Yuki reads the grounds afterward if it's quiet, and her readings are suspiciously kind.

Made by Yuki
How it's made ⌄
  1. One heaped teaspoon of flour-fine coffee per 70ml of cold water in the kanaka.
  2. Sugar goes in now, with the cold water — mazbout is half a teaspoon. Never sweeten afterward.
  3. Stir once while it's still cold, then put it on low heat and don't touch it again.
  4. Let the foam climb to the rim, pull it off the heat, let it settle. Do this twice more.
  5. Spoon the foam into the cup first, then pour. Wait a minute for the grounds to settle before you drink.

Sweet

Mochi Waffle

Crisp at the edges, impossibly chewy in the middle. Yuki makes twelve a day and stops. When they're gone she says "tomorrow" and means it as a comfort.

Made by Yuki
How it's made ⌄
  1. Whisk 200g glutinous rice flour, 40g sugar and 1 tsp baking powder. Glutinous rice flour only — regular rice flour gives you a sad biscuit.
  2. Beat in 1 egg and 240ml coconut milk until smooth.
  3. Rest the batter 20 minutes. This is where the chew comes from and skipping it is pointless.
  4. Hot, well-buttered waffle iron, 5–6 minutes — noticeably longer than feels right. You're waiting for the outside to go properly crisp.
  5. Twelve a day. When they're gone they're gone.
Hojicha Cake

Roasted tea in the sponge, barely any sugar in the cream. The slice people order thinking they'll share and then quietly do not share.

Made by Yuki
How it's made ⌄
  1. Grind 8g of hojicha leaf to a powder and sift it through 120g of flour.
  2. Cream 100g of soft butter with only 60g of sugar. The restraint is the recipe.
  3. Two eggs one at a time, then the flour, folded — not beaten — until it just comes together.
  4. 160°C for 35 minutes. The sponge should barely spring back; a fully bouncy one is overbaked.
  5. Top with 200ml cream whipped to soft peaks, with no sugar in it at all.
Sakura Slice

The one the café is named after. Pale pink, faintly salted, one preserved blossom on top. Yuki won't say what's in it. We've stopped asking.

Made by Yuki
How it's made ⌄

Yuki has never written this one down. What we've been able to gather from watching: a pale, tight-crumbed sponge; something salted folded through the cream; one preserved blossom placed on top with a pair of tweezers she keeps for nothing else. There is a step at the end she does with her back to the room. We have asked. She has smiled. That is where the matter rests.

Seasonal Something

Whatever Yuki found at the market that morning and couldn't stop thinking about. It changes without warning. It has never once been bad.

Made by Yuki
How it's made ⌄
  1. Go to the market early, before it's been picked over.
  2. Buy the thing you can't stop looking at. Not the thing on the list — there is no list.
  3. Get it home before deciding what it's going to become.
  4. Whatever it is, do less to it than you want to. It was already good in the crate.
  5. The recipe is written after the shopping. Never before.

Savory

Miso Croissant

Laminated with miso butter, so it's salty in a way that sneaks up on you. Best at 8am when they're still warm enough to fog the plate.

Made by Yuki
How it's made ⌄
  1. Beat 30g of white miso into 250g of cold butter and shape it into a flat block. Keep it cold — warm butter tears the dough.
  2. Make a standard croissant dough, chill it, then lock the miso butter inside.
  3. Three letter folds, resting an hour in the fridge between each. The dough decides the timing, not you.
  4. Roll, cut, shape, and prove 2½ hours at room temperature until they visibly jiggle when you nudge the tray.
  5. 200°C for 18 minutes. Eat at 8am while they still fog the plate.
Tamago Sando

Egg salad on soft milk bread, crusts off, cut clean. Absurdly simple. We have watched grown adults go quiet over this sandwich.

Made by Yuki
How it's made ⌄
  1. Six eggs, boiled 9 minutes, straight into ice water.
  2. Chop them rough, not fine. You want to find pieces of egg, not paste.
  3. Fold through 3 tbsp of kewpie mayonnaise, a pinch of sugar and salt. Nothing else — no mustard, no onion.
  4. Pile it generously onto soft milk bread. Cut the crusts off after assembling, never before.
  5. Wrap tightly and chill 20 minutes so it slices clean.
Rice & Whatever's Left

A bowl of rice and whatever the kitchen has at four in the afternoon. It's what the staff eat. It is not on the printed menu. Ask anyway.

Made by whoever's closest
How it's made ⌄
  1. Yesterday's rice, warmed in a pan with a splash of water and a lid on. Fresh rice is wrong for this.
  2. Open the fridge at four in the afternoon. Use what's actually there.
  3. Aim for one thing salty, one thing sharp, one thing soft. That ratio is the only real rule.
  4. A fried egg on top if there's an egg. There is usually an egg.
  5. Eat standing up, near the pass, in about four minutes.
Toast, Butter, Salt

For the people who come in at closing and say they're not hungry. They are always hungry. This is how we get around it.

Made by Hana
How it's made ⌄
  1. Good bread, cut thick. Thin toast is a different food and not the point.
  2. Toast it darker than you'd normally dare.
  3. Cold butter, sliced rather than spread, laid on while the toast is still hot so it half-melts and half-doesn't.
  4. Flaky salt. More than you think. Then a little more.
  5. Hand it over without commenting on the fact that they said they weren't hungry.

The Tuesday Morning Lecture.

Yuto's alarm goes off at 6:45 on Tuesday mornings.

Me3temed is always already awake.

He doesn't explain how. He is simply there — perched on the edge of the desk, watching the room with his soft half-open eyes, waiting for the morning to be officially acknowledged. When the alarm goes off and Yuto reaches to turn it off, Me3temed makes a small sound that means: finally.

By 7:15 they are both ready. Yuto in jeans and a clean shirt — the specific clothes of someone who is not quite a student and not quite a professor but something warmer than either. Me3temed in his café apron, which he refuses to remove even on university days, plus — for occasions of academic seriousness — a tiny pair of glasses that Yuto found for him in a curiosity shop in Khan el Khalili and which fit, against all reasonable expectation, perfectly.

They match. They have been told this. They do not disagree.

The drive to the university takes twenty minutes on a good Cairo morning, thirty-five on a Tuesday. They take it without complaint. Me3temed watches Cairo through the window the way he watches everything — quietly, completely, missing nothing. 🌿

The classroom smells like old books and whiteboard marker and the specific dusty warmth of a room that has held a lot of thinking.

Yuto loves it immediately, every time.

He sets his bag down. Opens his laptop. Writes one thing on the board before anyone arrives — not a topic, not an agenda. Just a question. The kind that doesn't have a clean answer and knows it.

The students come in slowly, the way students do at 9am — some still carrying coffee, some clearly having decided that presence counts as participation, a few already looking at him with the expression of people who came ready and are glad to be here.

Me3temed settles on Yuto's left shoulder. Adjusts his glasses. Surveys the room.

He has decided he is co-teaching. Yuto has not corrected this. 🦉

What Yuto loves about teaching — the thing he couldn't have explained before he started but now couldn't unknow — is the moment a room changes.

It doesn't always happen. Some sessions are good and practical and useful and that is enough. But sometimes — maybe once in a few weeks, maybe more — something shifts. A student says something that reframes the whole discussion. A question opens a door everyone was standing in front of without noticing. The room stops being a room full of people who showed up and becomes something else: a room full of people who are thinking together, following something, pulled forward by the same thread.

Those moments, Yuto has learned, cannot be manufactured. They can only be created conditions for.

So he creates conditions. He asks the strange questions. He leaves space after them. He doesn't fill the silence too quickly because he has learned that silence, attended to carefully, is often just a thought finishing its sentence.

Me3temed, on his shoulder, watches all of it with his steady eyes. Occasionally a student notices the small owl in the tiny glasses and loses their train of thought entirely. Yuto has factored this into the pacing. 🌸

By eleven the session is done. The students gather their things slowly — the ones who are in no rush to leave are, in Yuto's experience, always the ones who got something from it, even if they couldn't say what yet.

One student stops at the door.

I've been thinking about that question you asked last week, she says. I think I finally have something like an answer.

Tell me next time, Yuto says. I want to hear it.

She nods and goes. Me3temed watches her leave.

Good one, Me3temed says, in the way he says things — which is not in words, but in the specific stillness that means: yes. that was the right thing.

They stop at the café on the way back.

Not because they have to. Because it is where the day makes sense when they get back to it. Yuto unlocks the door. Puts the kettle on. Sits at table four for ten minutes before anyone arrives — the way Yuki does on Fridays, giving the space its five minutes to remember what it is.

Me3temed removes his academic glasses. Returns to his corner stool. Looks out the window at the Cairo afternoon with the expression of someone who has had a full morning and is exactly where he is supposed to be.

Yuto writes one thing in his notebook — not notes from the session, not a task, just a sentence. Something one of the students said that he wants to keep.

The café begins to fill.

The Tuesday continues.

Some days, Yuto thinks, are the kind that remind you why you chose everything you chose. The university in the morning, the café in the afternoon, the questions in between. The students who stop at the door. The small owl in the tiny glasses who watches it all and says nothing and means everything.

Those days, he has learned, are not rare.

They are Tuesdays.

They are always Tuesdays. 🌿🌸
